# PART C — THE PRODUCT, IN FULL

## 6. What Aurora is, and the goal behind it

### The one-line definition

Aurora is an installable, cross-platform web app that combines project boards, a personal daily planner, a unified calendar, a document editor, and a real-time collaborative whiteboard in a single workspace — priced per board rather than per person.

### The goal

Aurora exists to answer two specific frustrations that show up over and over in this category:

1. **Fragmentation fatigue.** People run their work across three or four tools — a board tool, a docs tool, a whiteboard tool, a calendar — and none of them agree with each other. Aurora's answer is one workspace where the calendar reads from the same cards and to-dos the boards do, and the whiteboard lives beside the docs.

2. **Overwhelm.** The most-cited reason people abandon tools in this category is not missing features — it's that the tool became too complicated to use. Aurora's answer is a written, enforced anti-bloat discipline (the Simplicity Guardrail, §8) that has actually overruled the founder's own feature ideas on the record.

### The commercial goal

Aurora is built to be a real, sellable product with sustainable unit economics at small scale — not a portfolio piece. Because fixed infrastructure cost is near zero (§12), Aurora is profitable at a customer count that would be a rounding error for a funded company. It does not need to win the category. It needs a durable, underserved slice of it.

### What Aurora is deliberately NOT

- Not an enterprise portfolio-management tool. No SSO, no governance layer, no custom-fields-at-scale. That buyer is not the target and never will be.
- Not an "everything app." Features are actively refused. Goals are a progress bar, not an OKR framework. Automations are three fixed behaviours, not a general rule builder.
- Not a native mobile app. It installs from any browser on any platform; there is deliberately no app-store presence (§11).

## 8. Complete feature inventory

Every feature below is shipped and live unless explicitly marked. Grouped as they'd be explained to a person, not as they're organised in the codebase.

### Group 1 — Where the day-to-day work happens

**Kanban boards** — the first thing every new user touches, and designed so every other feature can surface without leaving it.
- Columns with drag-and-drop cards
- Checklists inside cards, with progress bars and reorderable items
- Labels with a colour palette; create, attach, detach inline
- A priority tier per card (P1–P10 and beyond, open-ended)
- Due dates; **due *time*** as well as date on Pro
- Assignees, drawn from project members
- **Recurring cards** on a schedule — daily, weekly, monthly, or custom interval (the planner's recurrence engine, wired into boards on 17 Aug)
- **File attachments** (16 Aug) — private storage with signed URLs
- **Per-card time tracking** with start/stop and a running total; aware of multiple people on the same card; enforces one active timer per person account-wide
- Threaded comments with @mentions and reactions
- A review/approval workflow
- A per-card activity feed
- Card-level reminders; Pro gets custom offsets and channels
- Board toolbar with title search, due-date filter chips and label filter chips
- Confetti on completion (respects reduced-motion preferences)

**To-Do planner** — because not every task belongs on a shared board.
- A personal daily/weekly planner, separate from project boards
- Custom recurrence rules — daily, weekly, monthly, or arbitrary interval
- Priority tiers
- Bulk actions
- Swipe gestures on mobile
- Drag-and-drop reordering with fractional positioning; completed items auto-sink
- Starter templates, so a brand-new user has something useful the moment they open it
- *Aurora's own internal simplicity audit singles this page out as the best example of "zero-config default" in the product.*

**Calendar** — four views over one truth, not four systems pretending to agree.
- **Month**, **Week**, **Day** (added 22 Aug), and **Timeline/Gantt** (added 20 Aug)
- All four read from the same cards, to-dos and project milestones — there are no parallel data stores
- A project colour-key legend appears automatically once more than one project is in view
- Drag a card to a different day to reschedule it; drag or resize a timeline bar to change its span
- Quick-create directly from a day cell
- An agenda list view on small phones instead of a cramped grid
- **ICS subscribe feed (Pro)** — one-way, so a calendar app can see Aurora's dates

### Group 2 — Where the thinking happens

**Notes** — a block-based document editor, built on Tiptap.
- Headings, bullet lists, ordered lists, task lists
- Tables with per-column resize, and a floating toolbar that appears the instant the cursor enters a cell (add row above/below, add column left/right, delete row, delete column, toggle header, delete table with a confirm step)
- Image upload and clipboard paste, with resize
- Embeds
- **Math formulas** — KaTeX, inline and block, click-to-edit with a live preview, plus a tappable palette of ~28 LaTeX snippets (roots, fractions, integrals, sums, limits, matrices, Greek letters) for people who don't write LaTeX from memory
- Subscript and superscript
- **Auto-generated, clickable table of contents** per note (22 Aug) — anchors backfill onto notes written before the feature existed
- Folders
- Templates — curated starters plus "save this as a template"
- Full-text search across note content
- **Drag-and-drop block reordering**, plus keyboard shortcuts to move a block up or down as a single undo step
- Export to **Markdown** (free), **Word** (Pro), and **PDF** (Pro)
- A slash-command menu for inserting blocks and a `:` emoji autocomplete

**Canvas** — an infinite whiteboard that stays in sync live, with another person, on another device.
- Rendering via Konva; real-time sync via Yjs (a CRDT, so concurrent edits merge rather than clobber)
- Pressure-sensitive freehand drawing (perfect-freehand) with pen, marker and highlighter presets, a colour palette and a size slider
- Text boxes using the same editor schema as Notes — so formatting, math and tables work the same way in both places
- Images and media: upload, in-app recording, or embeds
- Tables
- Pages/frames with blank, ruled, grid or dotted backgrounds
- Z-ordering (bring to front / send to back / forward / backward), multi-select via marquee or shift-click, a layers panel with per-element visibility and lock toggles
- Copy/paste, duplicate, arrow-key nudging, right-click and long-press context menus
- Undo/redo
- **Live multiplayer cursors and presence**
- Palm rejection for stylus use
- Canvas is Pro-gated, and the code never ships the rendering library to users who don't have it — which also means the heaviest infrastructure cost is structurally tied to the paying accounts, not the free ones

### Group 3 — Where the team happens

**Collaboration**
- Owner / editor / viewer roles, enforced in the database via Row Level Security — not just by hiding buttons in the UI
- Email-keyed invitations, redeemed on the invitee's next sign-in
- Live presence — an avatar stack showing who's currently viewing the board
- Real-time sync across boards, cards, notes and members
- **A client-facing, no-account-required read-only share link** (16 Aug) — send a link, they see the board, they never sign up. Built directly in response to a documented freelancer need: showing a client progress without giving them a seat.
- Standalone note and personal-canvas sharing with its own role model (audited and bug-fixed 22 Aug — three UI call sites were ignoring the recipient's actual role; the underlying database rule had been correct throughout)

**Goals**
- Lightweight, flat progress-bar goals — a title, a target date, and progress either linked to a checklist or set as a percentage
- **Deliberately not an OKR system.** This is a Simplicity Guardrail decision on the record: the mitigation for scope creep was to make the feature smaller, not to put it behind a paywall. Shipped free.

**Automations**
- A small, fixed set of trigger/action rules (a 3×3 enum), Pro/Team gated
- Each rule renders as a plain sentence — "When a card moves to Done, assign it to…" — never as raw configuration
- **Deliberately not a general-purpose if-this-then-that builder.** The guardrail's own research names open-ended rule builders as the most direct path to the feature-overload complaint that drives churn in this category.

### Group 4 — Getting in, finding things, getting out

**Import**
- One-time board import from a competitor's JSON export or from CSV, into a new Aurora project
- One-way, no ongoing sync — the point is lowering the cost of *trying* Aurora, not building a two-way integration

**Search and command palette**
- Full-text search across card content and note content (17 Aug — before that, only names were indexed)
- Surfaced inside the same ⌘K palette used for jumping between projects, notes, canvases and folders
- One familiar affordance extended, rather than a second search interface bolted on

**MCP server — "Connect Claude"**
- A Pro or Team user can connect Claude Desktop or Claude Code directly to their own Aurora account and read or write boards, to-dos and notes through the same permission-enforced API the app itself uses
- Auth design: a personal access token, generated once and shown once; only its SHA-256 hash is stored; a database trigger blocks any writer to that column other than the service role; each tool call is authenticated by a short-lived (5-minute) token minted from a dedicated, independently revocable signing key — not the app's normal session key
- **This is genuinely unusual for a project-management tool at this stage**, and it's the single most distinctive feature for a technical audience. It's also flagged in the pentest brief as the highest-priority area to test.

### Group 5 — The quiet stuff that still matters

**Personalization**
- Day/Night themes, both first-class
- Font pairing (free) and custom colours (Pro)
- An 8-tile curated preset grid leads; a raw hex/colour-wheel picker is demoted into a collapsed "Advanced" disclosure — a deliberate rebuild after an earlier raw-picker-first version let people produce genuinely ugly results
- Synced to the account, not just the device, since 15 Aug

**PWA / installability**
- Installs on mobile and desktop from any browser
- Offline caching via a service worker
- Platform-appropriate install handling: a real native prompt on Chrome/Edge/Android; a guided walkthrough on iOS Safari, which exposes no programmatic install API

**Notifications and reminders**
- Due-date reminders by email (via Resend) and browser notification
- Pro adds custom timed reminders with configurable lead time and channel
- Sending domain: `mail.nvexis.com`, DKIM/SPF/DMARC verified

**Billing**
- Three self-serve tiers (Free, Pro, Team) plus an unpriced, manually-provisioned Enterprise tier for accounts past Team's 40-member cap
- Checkout, in-place plan switching, currency localization
- Entitlement is set only by a signature-verified webhook — the client-side redirect is never trusted — and a database trigger blocks any client write to billing columns

**Analytics (internal)**
- A minimal, privacy-respecting funnel-tracking layer shipped 15 Aug: seventeen allow-listed events, one table, no third-party vendor, no PII fields
- An admin-only dashboard at `/analytics` with funnel and breakdown views (23 Aug)
- Events tracked: `landing_page_viewed`, `signup_started`, `signup_completed`, `first_board_created`, `first_card_created`, `upgrade_prompt_shown`, `checkout_started`, `checkout_completed`, `install_prompt_shown`, `install_accepted`, `install_dismissed`, `import_completed`, `attachment_uploaded`, `automation_rule_created`, `table_inserted`, `time_entry_started`, `time_entry_stopped`
- UTM capture: `utm_source`, `utm_medium`, `utm_campaign`, `utm_term`, `utm_content` — **this is what makes every channel in §25 measurable**

### The Simplicity Guardrail — a feature in its own right

Every proposed feature is checked against a written document (`reports/SIMPLICITY-GUARDRAIL.md`) before it gets built. This is not aspirational: it has overruled its own advisory flags with a written reason on record (automations, goals and the Gantt view were each initially flagged as bloat risk, then explicitly greenlit with a stated mitigation), and it produced a real audit that found genuine overload in the *shipped* product, not just in new proposals.

This matters for marketing because it's a paper trail almost nobody in this category can show, and it's the credible half of the "calm" positioning. Anyone can *claim* their tool is simple. Aurora can show the document where it said no to itself.

## 9. Technology and security posture

### Stack

React · TypeScript (strict mode, no `any`) · Vite · Tailwind CSS · Framer Motion · dnd-kit (drag and drop) · Tiptap + Yjs (rich text and real-time) · Konva (canvas) · Supabase (Postgres, Auth, Row Level Security, Realtime, Edge Functions) · Cloudflare Pages (hosting) · Dodo Payments (Merchant of Record) · Resend (email)

### What is genuinely strong

- **Row Level Security is comprehensive, not decorative.** Every table is RLS-gated. Privileged operations run through `SECURITY DEFINER` functions with `search_path` pinned to empty — the correct defence against search-path hijacking. Billing fields and the calendar-feed token are each protected by dedicated database triggers that reject any writer except the service role.
- **Paid-feature boundaries are enforced twice.** Every gate (recurrence rules, member limits, project limits, canvas media caps) has both a client-side check and a database-level trigger or RLS rule. A client-only gate is trivially bypassable from browser devtools; Aurora doesn't have that hole anywhere it matters.
- **A real automated test posture.** Unit tests (Vitest), an accessibility suite (axe-core), and — unusual for a project this size — a pgTAP regression suite specifically proving multi-tenant isolation, gated in its own CI workflow.
- **Payment integrity follows the correct pattern.** The webhook is signature-verified and replay-protected via timestamp tolerance, and is the only writer of subscription state. The classic "client tells the server it paid" hole is closed.
- **A seven-phase security remediation** was completed 13–16 July 2026, with a separate verification phase.

### Honest gaps

## 15. Buyer psychology

### Pricing psychology already built into the product

- **Anchoring and the decoy effect.** Three visible tiers let Pro read as the obvious middle choice against Team's higher price. A two-tier free/paid layout converts worse.
- **Charm pricing.** $5.99 and $47.99 reliably outperform round numbers — the left-digit effect makes $5.99 register as meaningfully below $6.
- **Loss aversion via annual framing.** "33% off" triggers loss aversion more strongly than the equivalent "save $24 a year" framed as a gain.
- **The freemium foot-in-the-door.** A genuinely usable free tier — not a crippled trial — lowers first-use friction to near zero. For an unknown brand, asking for a card up front would kill most signups before anyone sees the product.

### What actually converts a free user to paid

1. **Trigger-moment upgrades beat time-based nags.** Someone who hits the 3-board or 2-collaborator limit *while trying to do something* converts far better than someone shown a generic upgrade banner on day three. Aurora's limits already double as natural triggers. **The actionable improvement: make the in-the-moment prompt name the specific blocked thing** — "Invite a third person to this board" rather than "Upgrade to Pro."
2. **Social proof closes the trust gap.** A brand-new product with zero reviews asks the buyer to carry all the risk. Even three specific, genuine testimonials outperform any amount of marketing copy, because they resolve the "will this work / will this still exist next year" anxiety that dominates a stranger's first thirty seconds. **Ask the first 20–30 real users explicitly. Put it in the plan (§25, week 4).**
3. **The "one thing that makes it obviously worth it" effect.** People rarely upgrade because they compared every feature. They upgrade because *one* gated feature became indispensable. Aurora now has several distinct candidates — time tracking, custom reminders, the canvas, exports, the calendar feed, the AI connector. **Marketing and onboarding should each surface a different single hero feature rather than selling the whole list at once.**
4. **Price sensitivity is not one curve.** A freelancer in one country, a student in another, and a six-person studio have entirely different reference prices. Regional pricing is the correct response and is already shipped. A student framing is worth testing later — extremely price-sensitive, but with unusually strong word-of-mouth within a campus.

### Why people switch tools in this category at all

Project and task management has unusually high tool churn. People are chronically dissatisfied with whatever they're using and actively browse alternatives. The two dominant switching triggers are:

- **Overwhelm** — the tool they have became too complicated. This is the single most-cited reason people leave the biggest tools in the category.
- **Fragmentation fatigue** — juggling three or four tools that don't talk to each other.

Aurora's promise answers both directly. **The strategic instruction that follows: lead with that promise explicitly, don't bury it under a feature list.**

# PART E — HOW WE TALK

## 19. Voice and tone — the rules

> **This is the most important section in the file.** An AI writing Aurora's copy will default to standard software-marketing language, and standard software-marketing language is exactly wrong here. Read this twice.

### The voice in one sentence

**Grounded, plain, honest, first-person. Short sentences. Says what happened and what it cost. Asks questions it actually wants answers to.**

### The seven rules

**1. Never announce. Just say the thing.**
The post does not open with a feeling about the news. It opens with the news, or with the problem the news solves.

> ❌ "Excited to share that Aurora now supports time tracking!"
> ✅ "Aurora tracks time per card now. You start a timer on the card you're working on, and it totals across everyone on that card."

**2. Be open that AI built a lot of this. Do not hide it, do not brag about it.**
Akash built Aurora working with AI coding agents. That is a *fact to state plainly*, not a boast and not a secret. The interesting content is in the *method* — the rules, the discipline, what went wrong — not in "look how fast AI is."

> ❌ "I built a full SaaS in 9 weeks solo with AI 🤯"
> ✅ "I built this working with AI coding agents, under a fairly strict set of rules — read the state doc before touching anything, update it after every change, commit immediately. The rules mattered more than the speed."

**3. Never brag about doing it alone.**
"Solo founder" as a badge is off-brand. If the fact that one person built it is relevant, state it as context or as a limitation, not as an achievement.

> ❌ "Built entirely by one person. No team. No funding."
> ✅ "It's one person and a lot of AI sessions, which is both why it moved fast and why support is going to be me for a while."

**4. Ask for feedback, and mean it.**
Nearly every post should have a real question at the end — one Akash actually wants answered. Not "what do you think?" as a comment-bait ritual. A specific question about a specific decision.

> ✅ "The free tier is 3 boards and 2 collaborators. I lowered it from 10 and 3 because the old limits let a whole small team run free forever. I'm still not sure 3 is the right number. If you've run a small team on a free plan — where did it start to pinch?"

**5. Concrete over abstract, always.**
Real numbers, real dates, real decisions. "56 database migrations" beats "a lot of work." "Mobile performance went from 63 to 70 and that still isn't good enough" beats "we improved performance."

**6. Admit what isn't done.**
Aurora has no independent security audit, no customers, no reviews. Saying so is not a weakness in the copy — it's the thing that makes the rest believable. Every post that admits something true earns the right to claim something else.

**7. If a word can be cut, cut it.**
No padding. No throat-clearing. No "in today's fast-paced world."

### Two registers

- **Founder voice** — first person "I". Used on the **personal LinkedIn profile** and on X. Warmer, more personal, more willing to say "here's what I got wrong." This is where most content lives, especially early.
- **Brand voice** — "we" or no pronoun. Used on the **Nvexis company page** and in product copy. Calm, clear, authoritative but humble. Less personal, still plain.

The two are not different personalities. The brand voice is the founder voice with the "I" taken out and the vulnerability turned down one notch.

### Formatting rules for social posts

- **No emoji as personality.** An occasional single emoji is tolerable; a decorative row of them is not. Default to none.
- **No rocket ships, no fire, no 100.** Ever.
- **No hook-line-then-blank-line-then-single-word paragraphs.** That's LinkedIn engagement-bait formatting and it undercuts everything above.
- Short paragraphs are fine. One-word paragraphs for drama are not.
- **No hashtag walls.** Zero to three hashtags, and only if they're genuinely a category (`#buildinpublic` is fine; `#innovation #growth #mindset` is not).
- Screenshots are good and should be used often — the product looks unlike anything else in the category and that does work by itself.
- Never fake engagement bait: no "comment YES for the link," no "DM me for early access" when there is no gate.

## 21. The no-competitor-names rule

**Rule, set by Akash, 29 August 2026: no competitor is named, ever, in any public marketing output.** Not in posts, not on comparison pages, not in ad copy, not in the Product Hunt description, not in replies to comments, not in "X alternative" framing. Not even descriptively, as in "a [brand]-style editor."

### Why this is a real constraint, stated honestly

This costs something, and you should know what. The highest-intent search traffic in this category is literally people typing a competitor's name plus "alternative." Refusing to use those names removes the single easiest SEO play available. §28 works around it with problem-led search terms instead, which is a slower and smaller channel, but it's compliant and it compounds.

**Do not quietly break this rule to make an SEO page work.** If it ever seems necessary, raise it with Akash as a decision (§35), don't just do it.

### Substitution vocabulary — use these instead

| Never write | Write instead |
|---|---|
| "a [brand]-style editor" | "a block-based document editor" · "docs that work in blocks" |
| "a [brand] alternative" | "another way to run your projects" · "if your current tool has got too heavy" |
| "like [whiteboard brand]" | "an infinite whiteboard" · "a shared canvas" |
| "unlike [brand], we don't charge per seat" | "the price doesn't go up when you add people" |
| "[brand] is overwhelming" | "most tools in this category keep adding surface area" · "the setup tax" |
| "migrate from [brand]" | "bring your existing boards in" · "import what you already have" |
| "cheaper than [brand]" | "$5.99 a month, flat, for up to ten people on a board" |
| "[brand] charges $X/seat" | "per-seat pricing means the bill grows every time someone joins" |

### The reframe that makes this easy

Every competitor comparison has a version that describes **the problem** rather than **the product**. The problem version is almost always the better copy anyway, because it's about the reader rather than about a company they may not even use.

> ❌ "Tired of [brand]'s cluttered interface?"
> ✅ "If you've ever opened your project tool and spent the first two minutes remembering where things are — that's the thing I was trying to avoid building."

**Post primarily from the personal profile for the first 60–90 days.** Reasons, in order of weight:

1. The company page has zero followers, so it has zero distribution. The personal profile's connections are the only real seed audience Aurora has.

**Always:** link the app, tag the Nvexis page where it's natural, and make sure a curious reader can get from a post to the product in one click.

## 30. Referral mechanic

**Build this in week 1.** It is the cheapest acquisition channel that exists and Aurora already has all the infrastructure.

### The mechanic

> Invite a collaborator to a board. When they sign up, you both get a month of Pro.

### Why it's nearly free

Invitations already exist in the product. This is copy and UI, not new architecture. The real marginal cost is **one month of forgone Pro revenue — roughly $5.99 gross, ~$5.23 net — paid to each of two people.** Compare that to a published blended referral acquisition cost of ~$310 across broader software, and a median self-serve acquisition cost of ~$702. At Aurora's ~$76/year customer value, this is the only channel whose arithmetic obviously works.

### Implementation notes

- Fire an analytics event on referral send and referral redemption so §32 can measure it.
- Put the prompt at a trigger moment — when someone is *already* inviting a person — not in a settings page nobody opens.
- Cap it sensibly to avoid abuse (e.g. a maximum number of referral months per account).

## 31. Paid spend — the gate

### The gate, both conditions required

1. **10+ real paying subscribers**, confirmed by the server-side `checkout_completed` event — never the client-side redirect.
2. **The independent penetration test complete**, with findings fixed.

Not a calendar date. If week 9 arrives and the gate isn't met, organic continues unchanged and the decision is revisited weekly.

### Why this gate and not a date

Paid acquisition before there's proof of what converts is spending blind. And spending to drive traffic at an unaudited product is the one scenario where a security incident lands at exactly the moment the most strangers are looking — which, at this stage, is brand-ending before the brand exists.

### First test, once gated: Reddit Ads

- **Why Reddit first:** by then there'll be weeks of organic Reddit participation showing which communities respond and what tone works. The groundwork is already laid.
- **2026 benchmarks:** CPC $0.50–$3.50 (most campaigns $0.75–$2.00), CPM $3–$12. Optimization guides recommend $1,500–3,000/month for statistically meaningful 30-day data.
- **Aurora's first budget: $150–300/month.** Deliberately far below that. This is a signal-of-life test, not a scaled campaign. **Expect noisy data** — that's the accepted trade for testing before there's real revenue to risk.
- **Why not spend at the ~$702 category median acquisition cost:** at ~$76/year customer value, that spend would take roughly a decade to recover. The budget is scrappy because the unit economics demand it, not because ambition is low.

### Measurement, end to end

UTM-tagged landing URL → `landing_page_viewed` → `signup_started` → `signup_completed` → `first_board_created` (activation) → `upgrade_prompt_shown` → `checkout_started` → `checkout_completed` (server-confirmed).

This entire chain is already instrumented. **Never evaluate a paid channel on clicks or signups alone** — a channel that drives signups which never create a board is worse than no channel, because it costs money and creates a false positive.
