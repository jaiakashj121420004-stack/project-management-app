01 — Executive Summary

Where Aurora stands, in one page

A fast, honest read of the whole picture before the detail.

Aurora is an installable, cross-platform Progressive Web App combining Kanban boards, a personal to-do planner with custom recurrence, a full calendar (now showing to-dos and project milestones, with an ICS subscribe feed), a Notion-style notes/canvas workspace, and real-time collaboration — built on React/TypeScript, Supabase (Postgres + Row Level Security + Auth + Realtime), and Dodo Payments as merchant of record. The product is unusually far along for a pre-revenue solo build — three pricing tiers, a hardened security posture (RLS on every table, rate-limited edge functions, a pgTAP regression suite, an internal remediation pass, and two prior strategy/pentest reports already on file), and a genuinely distinctive "calm, all-in-one, cheap" positioning against a crowded field of per-seat incumbents.

A snapshot as of 13 August 2026.

03 — Market Research

The market Aurora is entering

Size, growth, and where the real opportunity sits.

Independent research firms disagree on the exact size of the "project management software" market (a definitional problem more than a data problem — some include enterprise PPM/portfolio tools, others only SMB task tools), but they agree on direction: it is large, growing fast, and consolidating around all-in-one platforms rather than single-purpose tools.

Market size, 2026 (multi-source range)

Estimates for the 2026 global project management software market span roughly $7.3B to $12.2B, depending on scope definition. The mid-range consensus sits close to $10–11B.

Growth rate (CAGR)

Forecast CAGRs range from 10.6% to 18.5% depending on the forecast horizon (through 2030–2035); most mid-length forecasts (through ~2031–2033) cluster around 13–15.5% — a genuinely fast-growing category, well above general SaaS averages.

Why the market is growing this fast

  • Remote/hybrid work is now permanent infrastructure, not a pandemic artifact — distributed teams need a shared source of truth for work, which a shared doc or spreadsheet can't provide at scale.
  • Tool consolidation. Buyers are actively trying to cut the number of subscriptions per employee — the fastest-growing sub-segment is "all-in-one" workspaces (Notion, ClickUp, Monday) rather than single-purpose Kanban tools, which is exactly Aurora's positioning.
  • SMB and solo/freelancer adoption is accelerating faster than enterprise — smaller buyers are more price-sensitive and switch tools more readily, which favors a low-priced, fast-to-adopt product like Aurora over an enterprise-first incumbent.
  • AI-adjacent workflow tools are pulling more "planning" spend into software budgets generally, expanding the addressable pool of comparison shopping happening in this category.

Where Aurora actually fits

Aurora is not competing for the enterprise PPM buyer (Jira/Asana Enterprise/Monday Enterprise territory — governance, SSO, custom fields at scale). Its real addressable market is the bottom two-thirds of that $10B+ figure: individuals, freelancers, and small teams (2–10 people) currently paying $5–$15/seat/month for some combination of a board tool + a notes tool + a canvas tool, or paying nothing and limping along with spreadsheets and a free Trello board. That segment is large, underserved by any single "all-in-one but calm and cheap" product, and is exactly where the market-growth numbers above are concentrated.

Sizing the realistic slice: even capturing 0.001% of the ~$10–11B global market (a genuinely tiny, achievable share for a differentiated indie product with real marketing effort) implies roughly $100k in category spend redirected toward Aurora — several multiples of the Base-case revenue scenario in §12. The market is not the constraint; distribution execution is (see §8).

04 — Buyer Psychology

Why people actually pick (and pay for) a tool like this

The behavioral-economics research behind Aurora's pricing and messaging.

Pricing psychology already built into Aurora

  • Anchoring + decoy pricing. Three visible tiers (Free / Pro / Team) let Pro act as the anchored "obvious middle choice" against Team's higher price — a well-documented effect (the classic Economist-subscription decoy study) that increases mid-tier conversion versus a two-tier Free/Paid layout.
  • Charm pricing. $5.99 and $47.99 (vs. round numbers) reliably outperform round pricing in willingness-to-pay studies — the left-digit effect makes $5.99 register as meaningfully cheaper than $6, even though the gap is a cent.
  • Loss aversion via annual framing. Presenting the annual plan as "33% off" (a discount from a reference price) triggers loss aversion — skipping annual now feels like "losing" the discount, which is a stronger motivator than the equivalent "save $24/year" framing alone. Aurora already frames it this way in the pricing UI.
  • The freemium "foot in the door." A genuinely usable free tier (not a crippled trial) lowers the psychological barrier to first use to nearly zero — critical because for a low-trust, unknown indie brand, asking for a credit card up front would kill the vast majority of potential signups before they ever see the product.

What actually converts a free user to Pro

The behavioral research on freemium conversion (Nielsen Norman Group, OpenView, ProfitWell/Paddle data) converges on a few consistent drivers, all directly actionable for Aurora:

  • Trigger-moment upgrades beat time-based nags. A user who hits the 3-board limit or the 2-collaborator cap while actively trying to do something is far more likely to convert than one shown a generic "upgrade to Pro" banner on day 3. Aurora's limits already double as natural upgrade triggers — the opportunity is making the in-the-moment upgrade prompt copy speak to the specific thing being blocked ("Invite a 3rd person to this board" rather than "Upgrade to Pro").
  • Social proof closes the trust gap for an unknown brand. A brand-new SaaS with zero public reviews or testimonials asks buyers to take on all the risk. Even a handful of visible, specific testimonials ("I moved my whole freelance business off Notion+Trello+Miro into Aurora") outperform generic marketing copy, because they resolve the "will this actually work / will this company still exist next year" anxiety that dominates a stranger's first 30 seconds on a pricing page.
  • The "one thing that makes it obviously worth it" effect. For all-in-one tools specifically, conversion research shows users rarely upgrade because they compared every feature — they upgrade because ONE Pro-gated feature (for Aurora, most likely the canvas, custom reminders, or the ICS calendar feed) becomes something they personally can't live without. Marketing and onboarding should each surface a different single "hero" feature rather than trying to sell the whole feature list at once.
  • Price-sensitivity is not one curve. Willingness to pay differs sharply by segment and geography — a US freelancer, an Indian college student, and a 6-person creative studio have very different reference prices in their heads. Aurora's India-localized pricing (already built via Dodo) is the correct response to this; the same logic argues for eventually testing a "students" framing/discount, a segment with extremely high price sensitivity but strong word-of-mouth virality within a campus.

Category-specific psychology: why people switch PM tools at all

Unlike most SaaS categories, project/task management has unusually high "tool churn" — people are chronically dissatisfied with whatever they're using and actively browse alternatives, which shows up as strong search demand for "X alternative" and "best Y app" queries (see §10's SEO strategy). The two dominant switching triggers, per UX research on this category, are overwhelm (the incumbent tool became too complex — the #1 stated reason people leave Notion and ClickUp specifically) and fragmentation fatigue (juggling 3–4 separate tools for boards/notes/canvas/calendar). Aurora's brand promise — calm, all-in-one — is a direct answer to both, which is a genuine strategic advantage if the marketing message leads with it explicitly rather than burying it under a feature list.

06 — SWOT Analysis

Strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats

Grounded in the actual codebase, security posture, and market position — not generic SaaS boilerplate.

Strengths

  • Genuinely distinctive, cohesive design system (few indie tools this age have a real design language)
  • Flat per-board pricing undercuts every per-seat competitor for teams of 3+
  • Security posture is unusually mature for pre-revenue: RLS everywhere, pgTAP regression CI, prior internal remediation pass, pentest already scoped
  • Broad feature surface (boards + notes + canvas + calendar + recurrence) shipped by essentially one person, at real quality
  • Near-zero fixed infrastructure cost — Cloudflare/Supabase/Resend all free at current scale
  • India/PPP-aware pricing already built via Dodo, not a future to-do

Weaknesses

  • Zero product analytics/funnel instrumentation — no visibility into signup→activation→upgrade today (see §8)
  • Single-person (or very small) team — support, roadmap velocity, and incident response all bottleneck on one person
  • Zero brand awareness, zero reviews, zero social proof — full "unknown vendor" trust gap with buyers
  • Some legacy dead weight from the pre-rebrand era still in the dependency tree (old font packages — see §8/§9)
  • Preferences like theme/custom colors are per-device only, not synced — a real (documented) UX gap
  • No native mobile app presence (App Store / Play Store) — see §11 for the full tradeoff

Opportunities

  • Fast-growing category (~13–15% CAGR) with a large, underserved price-sensitive SMB/solo segment
  • "Tool fatigue"/overwhelm is the #1 stated churn driver from ClickUp/Notion users — a calm all-in-one is a direct answer, and a strong content/positioning angle
  • India and other PPP markets are large, price-driven, and already priced-for in the product
  • "Build in public" + indie-SaaS communities reward exactly this kind of transparent, design-forward solo build
  • The flat-per-board pricing story is a highly shareable, quotable marketing hook that's barely been used yet

Threats

  • Incumbents (Notion, ClickUp, Monday) have vastly larger marketing budgets and can bundle a "calm mode" feature to blunt Aurora's wedge at any time
  • Payment-processor/merchant-of-record fee or policy changes (Dodo) directly hit margin, given how thin unit economics are pre-scale
  • A single security incident, at this stage, could be brand-ending before the brand exists — this is precisely why the pentest in §8/§9 is not optional
  • Solo-founder key-person risk: support load, burnout, or life events halt the roadmap entirely with no backup
  • Platform risk from Apple/Google policy shifts if a native wrapper is ever built (see §11)
07 — Technology & Security Assessment

An honest read of the codebase

Grounded in this session's direct work inside the repository — not a generic checklist.

What's genuinely strong

  • Row Level Security is comprehensive, not decorative. Every table is RLS-gated; privileged operations run through SECURITY DEFINER functions with search_path pinned to empty (the correct defense against search-path hijacking) rather than trusting client-side checks. Billing fields and the new calendar-feed token are both protected by dedicated database triggers that reject any writer except the service role — a defense-in-depth pattern applied consistently, not just on the fields that seemed obviously risky.
  • The Pro-gating pattern is enforced twice, not once. Every paid-feature boundary (recurrence rules, member limits, project limits, canvas media caps/quota) has both a client-side UX gate and a database-level trigger or RLS check. A client-side-only gate is trivially bypassable via devtools; Aurora doesn't have that hole anywhere it matters.
  • A real automated test posture exists. Vitest unit tests, an axe-core accessibility suite, and — notably for a solo project — a dedicated pgTAP regression suite specifically proving multi-tenant isolation, gated in its own CI workflow. Most indie SaaS projects at this stage have none of this.
  • Payment integrity follows the correct pattern. The Dodo webhook (signature-verified, replay-protected via timestamp tolerance) is the only writer of plan/subscription state — the client-side checkout redirect is never trusted, closing the classic "client tells the server it paid" hole.

Real gaps worth fixing

  • No product analytics. There is no event tracking anywhere in the codebase — no funnel visibility into where free users drop off, which features actually drive upgrades, or which marketing channel a signup came from. This is a bigger risk to the business than any code-quality issue (expanded in §8).
  • Leftover dependencies from the pre-rebrand era. @fontsource-variable/inter and @fontsource-variable/space-grotesk are still listed in package.json, despite CLAUDE.md's explicit "Never Inter" brand rule and the Fraunces/Spectral/IBM Plex Mono system that replaced them. They aren't currently imported anywhere live, but a dead, unused dependency in a brand-sensitive codebase is exactly the kind of thing that gets accidentally reintroduced by a future contributor or an AI coding assistant reaching for a familiar font. Low effort, real risk-reduction: remove them.
  • The independent pentest is scoped but not commissioned. A prior internal report already identifies the right band ($4,000–$8,000, boutique/PTaaS firm, gray-box, multi-tenant-isolation-focused) — the risk is purely that it hasn't happened yet, and every day of real paid traffic before it does is exposure.
  • Local-only preference persistence. Theme, custom colors, and the onboarding-seen flag are all localStorage-only, not synced via the account. Documented as a deliberate scope cut, but it's a real, user-visible papercut ("why did my color scheme not follow me to my phone") once someone actually multi-devices.
  • Solo-maintainer bus factor. Not a code issue, but the single largest structural technical risk: essentially the entire system — architecture knowledge, the security model, the Dodo integration, the migration history — lives in one person's head plus the accumulated `memory.md`/`plan.md` docs. Worth writing a short "if I disappear tomorrow" runbook now, while it's cheap, not after it's urgently needed.

Code quality snapshot

TypeScript strict mode with no any, Zod validation on user input, small focused components, Conventional Commits, and a documented "golden workflow" (state doc → spec doc → build) are all actually followed in practice, not just declared in a CLAUDE.md nobody reads. The codebase reads as unusually disciplined for its size and single-operator origin — the honest technical risk here is entirely about business instrumentation and go-live process, not code craftsmanship.

08 — The Biggest Problem

If Aurora fails, here's the most likely reason why

Not a code bug. A visibility and distribution problem.

The biggest problem is not technical debt, security, or product quality — Aurora is unusually solid on all three for its stage. The biggest problem is that the business currently has zero ability to see or influence its own funnel, and zero distribution motion running, at the exact moment it's about to need both.

Concretely, two compounding gaps:

  1. No instrumentation. There is no analytics layer tracking signups, activation (did a new user actually create a board / add a card / open the canvas within the first session), feature usage, or which marketing source a user came from. Every dollar and hour spent on marketing in §10 will be spent blind — no way to know if Product Hunt drove signups that ever activated, whether the free-tier limits are the right level to trigger upgrades, or where users silently give up. This is fixable in days, not weeks, and should happen before the first real marketing push, not after.
  2. No live distribution motion yet. The product has been built to a high standard but has not been shown to a single external audience — no waitlist, no build-in-public following, no Product Hunt presence, no reviews, no backlinks. A great, secure, well-designed product with zero audience converts at exactly the same rate as a mediocre one: zero. The market research in §3 confirms the addressable market is large and growing; the psychology research in §4 confirms buyers in this category are actively shopping for alternatives right now. Neither of those facts generates a single signup on their own.

Put together, the real risk profile for Aurora is not "will it get hacked" or "will the code fall over" — both are reasonably well-guarded against already. It's "will anyone find out it exists before the founder's energy/runway for this project runs out." That reframes almost every prioritization decision from here: instrumentation and the first distribution motion (§10) should outrank any further feature work, including the polish items in §9 below.

11 — App Store & Play Store Feasibility

Should Aurora ship to the stores? Revisited with current facts

This section updates and reconciles two earlier internal conclusions that disagreed with each other.

The prior 16 July 2026 companion report recommended shipping to Google Play immediately via a Trusted Web Activity (TWA) wrapper. A later internal pricing review (12 August 2026) reversed that call, citing Google Play Billing fee-stacking on top of Dodo's merchant-of-record cut. Both conclusions were reasonable given what was known at the time — this section reconciles them with the current policy picture.

Google Play — technically easy, commercially the real question

Google officially and explicitly supports PWAs on Play via a Trusted Web Activity — this remains true and is Google's own recommended path for a PWA in 2026, confirmed in current documentation. The mechanical requirements (HTTPS, a valid manifest, a Digital Asset Links file, a Lighthouse PWA score, store assets) are all things Aurora already substantially meets.

The commercial catch is unchanged and confirmed: if a PWA distributed through Google Play sells digital goods or subscriptions, current Play policy requires those purchases to go through Google Play Billing (implemented via the Digital Goods API + Payment Request API inside the TWA) — not an external checkout like Dodo's. That would mean maintaining two billing systems (Dodo for web, Google Play Billing for Android-store users) and paying Google's cut (typically 15% for subscriptions after the first year, higher initially) in addition to Dodo's ~6–11% — a real margin hit exactly where Aurora's unit economics are already thin.

There is a narrower middle path worth knowing about: a TWA that simply links out to the existing web app, where all billing happens outside the Android app entirely (the user manages their subscription on the website, not "in-app"), can avoid triggering the Play Billing requirement — but Google's enforcement of exactly where that line sits has shifted over time (including recent US antitrust-driven changes allowing external purchase links in some circumstances) and is not something to rely on without care. This is a live policy area, not a settled one.

Apple App Store — the harder, more expensive path

This has not changed and is confirmed by current policy research: Apple's Guideline 4.2 requires an app to go meaningfully beyond "a repackaged website," and a bare PWA-in-a-WebView is routinely rejected. iOS also still does not support the install-prompt affordances Android/Chrome offer — every iOS PWA install remains a manual "Add to Home Screen," and Web Push for home-screen PWAs, while supported since iOS 16.4, is unavailable in the EU. Getting into the Apple App Store for real requires a genuine native wrapper (Capacitor, not a raw WebView) with real native functionality — push notifications, share sheet, biometric unlock — plus a $99/year developer account and real engineering time (realistically 1–3 weeks including one likely rejection-and-resubmit cycle).

Recommendation

  1. Stay web-only for now. The product already installs from any browser on any platform; app-store presence is a distribution and trust channel, not a requirement.
  2. Revisit Google Play once there are paying users, as a link-out-only TWA (no in-app billing) purely for Android discovery/trust — cheap, low-risk, and does not require touching the billing model.
  3. Treat Apple App Store as a Phase 2+ decision, gated on having a validated, paying user base and enough engineering slack to build a real Capacitor shell with genuine native features — not before.
  4. Do not integrate Google Play Billing unless Android-store distribution proves to be a major acquisition channel and the margin math is re-run at that point — right now it would add cost and complexity for an unproven benefit.
