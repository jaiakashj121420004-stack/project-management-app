01 — Executive Summary

Where Aurora stands, and the three questions this report answers

No number below is rounded in Aurora's favor without saying so.

Aurora (by Nvexis) is a multi-tenant, installable Progressive Web App for project management — Kanban boards, a daily to-do planner, a unified calendar, a Notion-style notes editor, and a real-time collaborative whiteboard canvas, plus a genuinely deep team layer: goals, fixed-rule automations, time tracking, file attachments, a client-facing share link, project templates, one-time Trello/CSV import, an MCP server that connects Claude Desktop/Code directly to a user's account, and funnel analytics. It runs on React 19 + TypeScript + Vite, Supabase (Postgres + Auth + Realtime + Storage + Edge Functions), Cloudflare Pages, and Dodo Payments as Merchant of Record — built and operated day to day by one person (Akash) working with AI coding agents under a documented engineering discipline (CLAUDE.md's "golden workflow"), with 56 database migrations and 13 Edge Functions shipped between 18 June and 22 August 2026.

"Is the product good? Is there a real edge? What will it realistically earn?" — the three questions a financially literate reader actually wants answered, in that order.
  • Is the product good? Yes, and unusually broad for a one-person team — §4 walks every feature area with the "why it matters," not a changelog dump. It also carries real, named weaknesses this report does not soften: a genuine solo-maintainer bus-factor risk that a runbook mitigates but doesn't remove, no admin dashboard for analytics, and zero brand awareness pre-launch — see §9. (Two code-completion gaps flagged when this report was first drafted — Task 31's drag-handle wiring and a pending Windows build pass — were closed and pushed to origin/main the same morning; see §3.)
  • Who's the competition, and is there a real edge? Trello, Asana, ClickUp, Monday.com, Notion, and Todoist all compete for the same buyer to varying degrees, and every one of their current prices was re-checked directly against their own pricing pages this session (§8) — not carried forward from a stale figure. Aurora's most defensible, quantifiable edge — flat per-board pricing instead of per-seat — is unmatched by any of them today.
  • What will it realistically earn, and how will it be marketed? A credible one-year target for a disciplined solo operator is low five figures in ARR, not a hockey stick (§13). The go-to-market plan (§14) is a real week-by-week calendar, not a bullet list — free/organic-first, with paid spend gated on Dodo confirming actual paying subscribers, not a date on a calendar.

What changed since the 13 August 2026 edition: a large amount of real product surface shipped in the intervening nine days — goals, automations, time tracking, file attachments, full-text search, project templates with a custom builder, Trello/CSV import, recurring cards, a client share link, the MCP connector, a Calendar Timeline/Gantt and Day view, table blocks, math formulas, note export to three formats, a funnel-analytics layer that didn't exist on 13 August, a written bus-factor runbook, and a scoped pentest plan. This edition doesn't just add those — it re-verifies every material figure from the prior report against a primary source, and corrects two of them outright (§8's Asana pricing, §13's India pricing) rather than repeating them.

What changed since the 22 August 2026 edition: four real, verifiable items closed in the day since — GitHub account MFA is now enabled (passkey, closing the bus-factor runbook's outstanding hardening item), the Resend sending domain (mail.nvexis.com) is DNS-verified and live (DKIM/SPF/DMARC in Cloudflare, retiring the onboarding@resend.dev fallback), and the admin analytics dashboard named as a gap in the prior edition has shipped — built, then two real bugs (an RLS-transaction-mode rejection and a Google-OAuth funnel blind spot) were found by actually loading it and fixed the same day, migration applied live. 02 — Team & Operating Model

Who's building this, and how

Short by design — this is what's actually verifiable, not a biography.

Aurora is built and operated by Akash, working solo with AI coding agents (Claude Code / Claude Cowork sessions) under a self-imposed, written engineering discipline rather than ad hoc vibes: every session reads the current state before touching anything, updates a living state document after every change, and commits immediately — the exact rule set is in CLAUDE.md and has been followed consistently enough to produce a real, traceable commit history rather than a black box. That process is the honest explanation for how one person shipped 56 migrations and 13 Edge Functions in nine weeks: it is not a claim that a solo operator moves as fast as a funded team on every axis, it's a specific, checkable claim about this operating model's output.

What this model is good at

Fast, coherent iteration on a single codebase with one voice deciding architecture — no cross-team coordination tax, no committee-designed features. The Simplicity Guardrail process (§8) is a direct product of this: a solo decision-maker can actually enforce a "no" consistently, which is a genuine, unusual advantage in a category whose biggest competitors are named for the opposite problem.

What this model is honestly exposed to

Everything bottlenecks on one person — support, roadmap velocity, incident response, and simple availability. This is named plainly in the SWOT (§9) as a real, unresolved weakness. The response so far is a written bus-factor runbook (§11) covering credential recovery, redeploy-from-scratch, and fire-drill triage — a mitigation, not a fix. There is no co-founder, no advisor structure, and no hiring plan disclosed in this report because none exists yet to disclose.

This report deliberately does not include a founder biography, prior-venture history, or credentials narrative — none of that is verifiable from the repository this report was built from, and per this report's own no-invented-numbers rule, it isn't included rather than guessed at.

04 — Product Overview

What Aurora actually does

Every area, in real detail — built by reading src/features/* directly, not from a marketing brief. Click any card to expand the full detail.

Where the day-to-day work happens

Kanban boards

The part of Aurora every first-time user touches first — and the part designed so every other feature can surface without leaving it.

Columns with drag-and-drop cards; checklists; labels; a priority tier per card; due dates, with due time (not just date) on Pro; assignees; recurring cards on a schedule (17 Aug — the to-do planner's recurrence engine, wired to boards); file attachments (16 Aug, the same private-Storage-plus-signed-URL pattern notes/canvas already used); per-card time tracking with start/stop and a running total, aware of multiple people on the same card and enforcing one active timer per person account-wide; threaded comments with @mentions and reactions; a review/approval workflow; a per-card activity feed; and card-level reminders, with Pro getting custom offsets and channels.

To-Do planner

Not every task belongs on a shared board — this is where personal work lives, on purpose.

A personal daily/weekly planner with custom recurrence rules (daily/weekly/monthly/interval), priority tiers, bulk actions, swipe gestures on mobile, drag-and-drop reordering with fractional positioning and auto-sink-on-complete, and starter templates so a new user has something useful the instant they open it. Aurora's own internal Simplicity Audit specifically calls this page out as doing "zero-config default" correctly.

Calendar

Four views over one unified truth — not four separate systems pretending to agree.

Month, Week, Day (added 22 Aug), and a Timeline/Gantt view (added 20 Aug) — all reading from the same cards, to-dos, and project milestones rather than four parallel data stores. A project color-key legend appears once more than one project is in view. A Pro-gated ICS subscribe feed lets Google/Apple/Outlook see Aurora's dates one-way. Competitor research (§8) calls this exact feature a bolt-on when it exists at all elsewhere; Aurora's version is a first-class, unified surface.

Where the thinking happens

Notes

A Notion-style editor with the one thing most Notion competitors skip: it stays fast and legible even as a note grows.

Built on Tiptap: headings, bullet/ordered/task lists, tables with per-column resize, image upload and clipboard paste, embeds, math formulas (KaTeX, inline and block, click-to-edit with a live preview), subscript and superscript, an auto-generated clickable table of contents per note (22 Aug, with anchors that lazily backfill onto notes written before the feature existed), folders, templates (curated and "save as template"), full-text search, drag-and-drop block reordering (wired live 22 Aug, commit 1db9282), and export to Markdown, Word (Pro), and PDF (Pro).

Canvas

An infinite whiteboard that stays in sync live, with a second person, on a different device, in real time.

Konva for rendering, Yjs for the CRDT sync layer, perfect-freehand for pressure-sensitive strokes: freehand drawing, text boxes, images and media (audio/video, in-app recording, upload, or YouTube/Vimeo/Loom/SoundCloud embeds), tables, pages/frames, z-ordering, multi-select, a layers panel, and undo/redo, with live multiplayer cursors and presence. Most competitors studied in §8 either bolt this on or omit it entirely; Aurora ships it as a native, Pro-gated primitive.

Where the team happens

Collaboration

Roles enforced where a client can't fake them — the database — not just hidden buttons in the UI.

Owner/editor/viewer roles enforced in Postgres Row Level Security; email-keyed invitations; live presence; real-time sync; a client-facing, no-account-required read-only share link for boards (16 Aug — the direct answer to a documented freelancer need: showing a client progress without giving them a seat); and standalone note/personal-canvas sharing with its own role model, audited and bug-fixed for real on 22 Aug (three UI call sites were silently ignoring the recipient's actual role — the underlying database rule was correct throughout).

Goals

A progress bar, not an OKR framework — a deliberate refusal to build the enterprise version nobody here asked for.

Lightweight, flat progress-bar goals — deliberately not an Objectives/Key-Results system. A conscious Simplicity Guardrail call, shipped free rather than Pro-gated because the mitigation is scope, not a paywall (§8).

Automations

Three fixed behaviors, zero configuration screens — the guardrail's single clearest self-restraint.

A small, fixed trigger/action rule set, Pro/Team-gated — deliberately not a general-purpose "if X then Y" rule builder, which the guardrail's own competitor research names as the single most direct path to the "feature overload" reviewers cite as ClickUp's top churn driver.

Getting in, finding things, and getting out

Import

A one-way door out of a competitor's tool and into Aurora — the switching-cost killer.

One-time Trello JSON export / CSV import into a new Aurora project — one-way, no ongoing sync, aimed squarely at lowering the cost of trying Aurora over an incumbent.

Search & command palette

"Where did I write that thing about the Q3 deadline" — now actually answerable.

Full-text search across card and note content (17 Aug — previously the command palette only indexed names), surfaced inside the same ⌘K palette used for quick navigation across projects, notes, canvases, and folders. One familiar affordance extended, not a second search UI.

MCP server — "Connect Claude"

A genuinely unusual feature for a project-management tool at this stage — worth real explanation, not a throwaway line.

A Pro+ user can connect Claude Desktop or Claude Code directly to their own Aurora account via a personal access token, and read or write boards, to-dos, and notes through the same RLS-enforced API the app itself uses. The token is generated once, shown once, and only its SHA-256 hash is ever stored; a database trigger blocks any writer to that column other than the service role. Each tool call is authenticated by a short-lived (5-minute) JWT minted from a dedicated, independently-revocable signing key — not the project's normal session key. It lets a technical user (§5's third persona) manage their boards from inside an AI coding session instead of a browser tab, and it's real, novel authentication infrastructure the pentest brief (§10) flags as its highest-priority area to test.

The quiet stuff that still matters

Personalization

Curated by default, raw control one tap away — the settings page as a case study in the guardrail working.

Day/Night themes, font pairing, and custom color presets — an 8-tile curated grid leading, with a raw hex/color-wheel picker demoted to a collapsed "Advanced" disclosure (a deliberate rebuild after an earlier raw-picker version produced ugly, uncoordinated results) — synced to the account, not just the device, since 15 Aug.

PWA / installability

Installs like a real app, on every platform, from a browser — no app-store gatekeeper.

Installable on mobile and desktop from any browser, offline caching via a service worker, and platform-appropriate install-prompt handling — a real native prompt on Chrome/Edge/Android, a guided walkthrough on iOS Safari, which exposes no programmatic install API.

Notifications & reminders

Nothing slips, even with the app closed.

Due-date reminders via email (Resend) and browser notification; Pro gets custom timed reminders with configurable lead time and channel.

Billing

The server is always the source of truth for who's paid — never the client, never a hidden trust assumption.

Three priced, self-serve tiers (Free/Pro/Team) plus an unpriced, manually-provisioned Enterprise tier for accounts past Team's 40-member cap; Dodo Payments checkout; in-place plan switching; currency localization; and webhook-verified entitlement, enforced by a database trigger that blocks any client write to billing columns.

Analytics (internal)

The instrument panel nobody sees but the founder — and the thing that closes last edition's biggest named risk.

A minimal, privacy-respecting funnel-tracking layer shipped 15 Aug — seventeen allow-listed events, one table, no dashboard, no third-party vendor, no PII fields, its real audience internal rather than customer-facing. It's the instrumentation behind this report's revenue and marketing modeling (§13, §14) — closing the prior edition's biggest named gap.

Why the market is growing this fast

  • Remote/hybrid work is now permanent infrastructure, not a pandemic artifact — distributed teams need a shared source of truth a spreadsheet can't provide at scale.
  • Tool consolidation. The fastest-growing sub-segment is "all-in-one" workspaces over single-purpose Kanban tools — Aurora's exact positioning.
  • SMB and solo/freelancer adoption is accelerating faster than enterprise, favoring a low-priced, fast-to-adopt product over an enterprise-first incumbent.
  • AI-adjacent workflow tools are pulling more "planning" software spend into comparison shopping generally.

Where Aurora actually fits

Aurora is not competing for the enterprise PPM buyer (Jira/Asana Enterprise/Monday Enterprise territory — SSO, governance, custom fields at scale). Its real addressable market is the bottom two-thirds of that $9–12B figure: individuals, freelancers, and small teams (2–10 people) currently paying $5–$15/seat/month for some combination of a board tool, a notes tool, and a canvas tool — or paying nothing and limping along with spreadsheets and a free Trello board.

Sizing the realistic slice: even capturing 0.001% of the ~$9–12B global market implies roughly $90k–$120k in category spend redirected toward Aurora — several multiples of even the Best-case revenue scenario in §13. The market is not the constraint; distribution execution is (§14).

07 — Buyer Psychology

Why people actually pick, and pay for, a tool like this

The behavioral research behind Aurora's pricing and messaging.

Pricing psychology already built into Aurora

  • Anchoring + decoy pricing. Three visible tiers let Pro act as the anchored "obvious middle choice" against Team's higher price.
  • Charm pricing. $5.99 and $47.99 reliably outperform round pricing in willingness-to-pay studies.
  • Loss aversion via annual framing. "33% off" triggers loss aversion more effectively than "save $24/year" framed as a gain.
  • The freemium "foot in the door." A genuinely usable free tier lowers first-use friction to near zero — critical for an unknown indie brand.

What actually converts a free user to Pro

Trigger-moment upgrades beat time-based nags; social proof closes the trust gap for an unknown brand; users upgrade because one Pro-gated feature becomes indispensable, not because they compared the whole feature list. The feature set now offers meaningfully more of these "one thing" hooks than it did on 13 August — time tracking, custom reminders, canvas, automations, templates, and exports are each a real, distinct reason a specific user might hit "upgrade." This is a genuine, citable reason to expect Pro-conversion assumptions might land modestly higher than the 13 August estimate — §13 treats it as an explicit judgment call, not a measured fact.

Category-specific psychology: why people switch PM tools at all

Project/task management has unusually high "tool churn" — the two dominant switching triggers are overwhelm (the #1 stated reason people leave Notion and ClickUp) and fragmentation fatigue (juggling 3–4 separate tools). Aurora's calm, all-in-one brand promise answers both directly, and per this session's fresh competitor research (§8), no major competitor has shipped a named "calm mode" or decluttering feature that would blunt this positioning as of 22 August 2026.

Security model

Row Level Security is enabled on every table, and every per-project table's policy resolves membership through a SECURITY DEFINER helper function rather than a sub-query — the pattern that avoids Postgres's known RLS self-recursion failure mode. A 7-phase internal remediation (closed 14–16 July 2026) covered privilege-escalation hardening, SECURITY DEFINER search_path pinning, billing-PII isolation from co-members, and Edge Function hardening. Two automated pgTAP regression suites run in CI on every migration: the core RLS suite (15 assertions) and a newer one covering an information-disclosure fix (4 assertions) — 19 assertions total, proving a non-member can't read or write another tenant's data, a viewer can read but not write, and a user can't self-upgrade their own plan column.

A pre-pentest hardening pass on 16 August 2026 found one real issue before vendor handoff: two BEFORE INSERT triggers checked a target row's plan/usage before Postgres evaluated the row's own RLS check clause, letting a caller who knew another user's id distinguish "this account is at its limit" from "headroom exists" purely from an error message — a minor information-disclosure bug (an authorization oracle, not a data-write vulnerability; the insert itself always failed for a non-owner). Fixed with a dedicated migration and a 4-assertion regression test proving the fix.

MCP server auth — the newest, least-battle-tested surface

Aurora's own pentest readiness brief ranks the MCP connector as the highest-priority area for an external tester, ahead of standard web classes, because it's genuinely novel token-minting infrastructure with no precedent elsewhere in the app: a 32-random-byte token, hashed and stored; a trigger that rejects any writer to the hash column other than the service role; and a 5-minute-TTL JWT minted per tool call from a dedicated signing key, so RLS — not custom access-control code — decides what a connected Claude session can touch.

Pentest — scoped, budgeted, and scheduled, not a gap

Per the founder's explicit framing, this is a planned pre-launch action (§11), not a weakness. Scope (gray-box, ~one app plus its PostgREST/RPC surface, two-to-three tenant accounts across plan tiers) and budget ($4,000–$8,000) are already decided; commissioning a vendor is what remains.

That's a real, quantifiable, near-zero cost per acquisition, and it belongs in Phase 1 as a build item, not a Phase 2 afterthought.

15 — App Store & Play Store Feasibility

Should Aurora ship to the stores? Re-verified with current facts

Carries forward the 13 August conclusion; re-checked against 2026 policy research this session.

Google Play — technically easy, commercially the real question

Google continues to officially support PWAs on Play via a Trusted Web Activity (TWA) wrapper. The commercial catch is unchanged: a PWA distributed through Google Play that sells digital goods/subscriptions must route those purchases through Google Play Billing — not an external checkout like Dodo's — which would mean maintaining two billing systems and paying Google's cut (typically ~15% for subscriptions after year one) in addition to Dodo's fee, a real margin hit on already-thin unit economics (§13). A narrower middle path — a TWA that links out to the web app, with billing staying external — can avoid triggering the Play Billing requirement, but 2026 research this session found continued regulatory movement (external-purchase-link allowances tied to antitrust rulings) that keeps this a live, evolving policy area rather than a settled one.

Apple App Store — the harder, more expensive path

Unchanged: Apple's Guideline 4.2 requires an app to go meaningfully beyond "a repackaged website," and a bare PWA-in-a-WebView is routinely rejected. iOS still lacks Android/Chrome's native install-prompt affordance. A real App Store presence would require a genuine native wrapper (Capacitor, not a raw WebView), a $99/year developer account, and real engineering time.

Recommendation — unchanged from 13 August

  1. Stay web-only for now. The product already installs from any browser on any platform; app-store presence is a distribution/trust channel, not a requirement — and the founder has already confirmed this for the current improvement-plan cycle.
  2. Revisit Google Play once there are paying users, as a link-out-only TWA (no in-app billing).
  3. Treat Apple App Store as a Phase 2+ decision, gated on a validated paying user base.
  4. Do not integrate Google Play Billing unless Android-store distribution proves to be a major acquisition channel and the margin math is re-run at that point.
16 — Investor FAQ

The questions a skeptical reader will actually ask

Answered directly, including where the honest answer is "not yet."

Why won't Notion, ClickUp, or Asana just copy the flat-pricing or "calm" positioning?

They could, and §9 lists it as a real threat, not a dismissed one. But flat per-board pricing is structurally awkward for a company whose entire revenue model is per-seat — it's a cannibalization decision, not a feature toggle, which is why none of the six competitors studied have moved on it despite it being a knowable, public gap for months. "Calm mode" is easier to copy technically but harder to copy credibly: it would read as a direct admission that the overwhelm complaints are true, from companies whose whole roadmap has been adding features. Neither is impossible, both are genuinely harder for an incumbent than for Aurora to do first.

What happens if the founder can't continue?

A written bus-factor runbook exists — credential locations, a from-scratch redeploy procedure, third-party contacts, and fire-drill triage steps, all documented outside any one person's head. That materially shortens a hypothetical successor's ramp-up time. It does not solve the underlying single-point-of-failure risk (§9 names this plainly), and there is no successor, co-founder, or contingency staffing plan in place today.

Is this pre-revenue with real traction, or just an idea with a landing page?

Pre-revenue, but not pre-product. §3 and §10's evidence (56 migrations, 13 Edge Functions, a completed 7-phase security remediation, 19 passing regression assertions) is code-verifiable, not marketing claims. Zero paying customers and zero marketing spend to date are stated plainly (§00 Snapshot) rather than obscured — the product risk is largely retired; the distribution risk is entirely ahead, which is exactly what §14 exists to address.

How defensible is a solo-built product against funded competitors with real marketing budgets?

Not defensible on marketing spend — that's conceded outright in §9's Threats. The defensibility argument is narrower and more specific: near-zero fixed cost (§12) means Aurora can be profitable at a customer count that would be a rounding error for a funded competitor, so it doesn't need to win the category, only a durable, underserved slice of it (§6). That's a real strategy, not a hope, but it is genuinely a different bet than "outcompete ClickUp."

What's the single biggest risk to this thesis?

Distribution, not product or cost. §13's honest-expectations note says it plainly: most independent SaaS never crosses $1k MRR, and that's historically a distribution failure. Every other risk in §9's register is medium-likelihood at worst; this one is the whole game.

Is the India/PPP pricing story real?

Partially confirmed, honestly flagged. Dodo's currency-localization infrastructure is real and verified (§13). Whether Aurora has actually configured a deliberately discounted India price, versus a straight FX conversion of the US price, could not be confirmed from the codebase this session. This is presented as a concrete, cheap, near-term fix, not a structural problem — see §13 and §11's checklist.
